\subsection{Matrix-free rSVD}
\subsubsection{Asymptotic analysis}
To explain the computational times and asymptotic behavior in Sections~\ref{sec:computational_time} and \ref{sec:computational_breakdown}, we examine the cost of forming $K$ and of Algorithm~\ref{alg:discrete_rsvd} in more detail.

For a 2D elliptic problem, the Cholesky factorization of $A$ and $M_\partial$ can be done in $\mathcal{O}(N^{3/2})$ and $\mathcal{O}(N_b^{3/2})$ computations, respectively (Section~\ref{sec:initial_matrix_factorizations}). The resulting triangular systems can be solved at the respective costs $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$ and $\mathcal{O}(N_b \log N_b)$. Since $N_b \ll N$, we may disregard the factorization and triangular solves using $M_\partial$. 

The cost of computing $K = T A^{-1} M$ involves solving $N_b$ linear systems $A v_i = T^T e_i$ (Section~\ref{sec:approximating}), each system costing $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$ using a sparse Cholesky factorization of $A$. With $N_b \sim \sqrt{N}$, the cost therefore scales as $\mathcal{O}(N^{3/2} \log N)$, explaining the scaling of $t_K$ observed in the left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:rsvd_computational_time_comp}. The SVD of dense matrix $K \in \mathbb{R}^{N_b \times N}$ costs $\mathcal{O}(N_b^2 N) = \mathcal{O}(N^2)$ \cite{golubMatrixComputations4th2013}, which is larger than $\mathcal{O}(N^{3/2} \log N)$, explaining $t_{\text{SVD}}$ in Figure~\ref{fig:rsvd_computational_time_comp}.

For the matrix-free rSVD (Algorithm~\ref{alg:discrete_rsvd}), factorizing $A$ costs $\mathcal{O}(N^{3/2})$, and is done once. Each application of $K$ and $K^*$ costs $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$, for a total of $\mathcal{O}(l N \log N)$. The SVD of the $l \times N$ matrix $B$ costs $\mathcal{O}(l^2 N)$ \cite{golubMatrixComputations4th2013}, and the cost of the QR factorization of the small $N_b \times l$ matrix $Y$ is $\mathcal{O}(l^2 N_b)$ \cite{golubMatrixComputations4th2013}, and may be disregarded. Hence, assuming two dimensions and an efficient sparse Cholesky factorization of $A$, Algorithm~\ref{alg:discrete_rsvd} costs
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rsvd_cost}
    \underbrace{\mathcal{O}(N^{3/2})}_{\text{setup}}
    + \underbrace{\mathcal{O}(l N \log N)}_{\substack{\text{forward and} \\ \text{adjoint solves}}}
    + \underbrace{\mathcal{O}(l^2 N)}_{\text{SVD of } B}.
\end{equation}

This is consistent with the results in the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:rsvd_computational_time_comp}: $t_{\text{rSVD}}$ scales near-linearly on the observed range, matching the near-linear scaling of $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$. Even if $\mathcal{O}(N^{3/2})$ dominates $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$ at large $N$, the hidden constant of the big-O notation makes the scaling be more similar to $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:rsvd_computational_breakdown}), explaining the near-linear scaling in Figure~\ref{fig:rsvd_computational_time_comp}.

Equation~\eqref{eq:rsvd_cost} also explains the cost breakdown in Figure~\ref{fig:rsvd_computational_breakdown}. For larger $k$, a larger share is taken up by the SVD stage, as predicted by $\mathcal{O}(l^2 N)$. For $k = 200$ ($l = k + p$), the largest considered target rank, we observed that as $N$ increases, the forward and adjoint solves start taking up a larger share of the time compared to the SVD of $B$, also consistent with Equation~\eqref{eq:rsvd_cost}. The cost $\mathcal{O}(l^2 N_b)$ of the QR-factorization $QR = Y$ is negligible, matching the results for all $k$ and $N$.


\subsubsection{Overhead of the adjoint formulation}
The adjoint formulation of Algorithm~\ref{alg:discrete_rsvd_Kstar} has some computational overhead compared to Algorithm~\ref{alg:discrete_rsvd}. The setup stage requires an additional Cholesky factorization of the $N \times N$ matrix $M$, which costs $\mathcal{O}(N^{3/2})$. For the QR factorization, $Y$ is now $N \times l$, so this costs $\mathcal{O}(l^2 N)$, compared to $\mathcal{O}(l^2 \sqrt{N})$ for rSVD on $K$. Computing $B = Q^T K^*$ requires solving $M \tilde q_i = q_i$ $l$ times, costing $\mathcal{O}(l N \log N)$ (compared to $\mathcal{O}(l \sqrt{N} \log \sqrt{N})$, which was negligible). The SVD of $B \in \mathbb{R}^{l \times N_b}$ costs $\mathcal{O}(l^2 \sqrt{N})$, which is cheaper than $\mathcal{O}(l^2 N)$ for rSVD on $K$. Overall, the asymptotic cost of Algorithm~\ref{alg:discrete_rsvd_Kstar} is the same as Algorithm~\ref{alg:discrete_rsvd}, which is given in Equation~\eqref{eq:rsvd_cost}.

In Table~\ref{tab:computational_time_adjoint} ($t_1/t$), we see approximately 30\% computational overhead for $k = 10$, 20\% for $k = 50$, and 10\% for $k = 200$. This matches the theoretical overhead, which reduces for larger $k$ when the cost $\mathcal{O}(l^2 N)$ of the SVD in Algorithm~\ref{alg:discrete_rsvd} takes up a larger share.

To recover the SVD of $K$ from the SVD of $K^*$ (Section~\ref{sec:reconvering_the_svd}), QR factorizations of $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N_b \times k}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times k}$ costs $\mathcal{O}(k^2 \sqrt{N}) + \mathcal{O}(k^2 N) = \mathcal{O}(k^2 N)$ \cite{golubMatrixComputations4th2013}. The SVD of $R_A \bar \Sigma R_b \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}$ is $\mathcal{O}(k^3)$ \cite{golubMatrixComputations4th2013}, which is negligible compared to $\mathcal{O}(k^2 N)$, since $k \ll N$. This is in correspondence with the reported computational overhead $t_2 / t$ in Table~\ref{tab:computational_time_adjoint}: as $k$ increases, the extra cost $\mathcal{O}(k^2 N)$ of the recovery procedure and the reduced cost $\mathcal{O}(l^2 \sqrt{N})$ of the SVD of $B$ balance out, leading to approximately 30\% extra computational time using rSVD on $K^*$ and recovery of the SVD, compared to the direct approach of rSVD on $K$.


\subsubsection{Analysis of reconstruction error}\label{sec:analysis_of_reconstruction_error}
At target rank $k = 50$, rSVD reconstruction errors were within 0.5\% to 4\% of the exact Tikhonov solution in Euclidean norm and AUC-IoU (4\% to 39\% in EMD), with visually indistinguishable reconstructions (Section~\ref{sec:target_rank_reconstruction}, Figure~\ref{fig:test_problems_selected}).

To understand these results, we inspect the structure of $Y = K \Psi$. Following the setup of Halko et al. \cite[Sec.~9]{halkoFindingStructureRandomness2011}, let $K = U \Sigma V^T$ be the SVD of $K$, and partition $\Sigma$ as $\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\Sigma_1,\, \Sigma_2)$, where $\Sigma_1$ contains the $k$ first singular values of $K$, and $\Sigma_2$ the remainder. Correspondingly, partition the right singular values as $V = [V_1\,\, V_2]$, and write $K$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rsvd_partitioned_1}
    K = U \begin{bmatrix}
    \Sigma_1 & \\ & \Sigma_2
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
    V_1^T \\ V_2^T
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Right-multiplying by $\Psi$ and setting $\Psi_1 = V_1^T \Psi$ and $\Psi_2 = V_2^T \Psi$ gives
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:rsvd_partitioned_2}
    K = U \begin{bmatrix}
    \Sigma_1 \Psi_1 \\
    \Sigma_2 \Psi_2
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
Intuitively, $\Sigma_1 \Psi_1$ represents the actions of $K$, while $\Sigma_2 \Psi_2$ represents the perturbation. By Theorem~10.5 in \cite{halkoFindingStructureRandomness2011}, if $\Psi \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times l}$ is a random Gaussian matrix, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rsvd_error_bound}
    \mathbb{E}\| K - K_k \|_F \le
    \gamma_k \sqrt{\sum_{i = k+1}^{N_b} \sigma_i^2},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{E}$ denotes the expected value, $\gamma_k = \sqrt{1 + k / (p - 1)}$, and $K_k$ is the rank-$k$ approximation obtained from Algorithm~\ref{alg:discrete_rsvd}.

When the singular values of $K$ decay rapidly, the perturbation $\Sigma_2 \Psi_2$ remains low and the sum in Equation~\eqref{eq:rsvd_error_bound} is small, resulting in a good rank-$k$ approximation. If the singular values decay more slowly, $\sum_{i=k+1}^{N_b} \sigma_i^2$ will be larger, and the expected error increases.

Since $K$ has rapidly decaying singular values
(Figure~\ref{fig:singular_values}), a target rank
$k \ll \operatorname{rank}(K) = N_b$ yields an accurate rank-$k$
approximation: the main actions of $K$ are captured in $\Sigma_1 \Psi_1$. This argument transfers to the weighted Tikhonov problem in Equation~\eqref{eq:discrete_inverse_problem}. Both $K$ and $W$ are approximated accurately at $k = 50$, keeping the reconstruction error within 4\% (Euclidean, AUC-IoU). Since the random sampling introduces some noise, the EMD shows a higher error, as it is more sensitive to low-amplitude noise.


\subsubsection{Reconstruction error using the adjoint operator}
\input{figures/true_singular_values.tex}
Using rSVD on $K$ gives slightly lower reconstruction error than rSVD on $K^*$ (Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction_error_using_the_adjoint_operator}). The quality of the random sampling $Y = K\Psi$ versus $Y' = K^* \Psi'$ depends on the spectral decay of the operators. Figure~\ref{fig:true_singular_values} shows that the singular values of $K^*$ have a slower decay than those of $K$, and we therefore expect a higher approximation error by Equation~\eqref{eq:rsvd_error_bound}. The difference originates in the mass matrix factors of the relation $K^* = M^{-1} K^T M_\partial$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:K_star_def}).


\subsubsection{Effect of the test vector distribution}
\input{figures/singular_vectors_of_K.tex}
Some test vector distributions slightly reduce reconstruction error, with the ``Peak'' test vectors giving lower EMD than Gaussian or Rademacher (Section~\ref{sec:test_vector_distribution_effect_on_error}).

Consider a single sample $y = K \psi = U \Sigma V^T \psi$. In the best case, $\psi = a v_1$ with $a \neq 0$: then $V^T(a v_1) = a e_1$, $K(a v_1) = a \sigma_1 u_1$, and the QR factorization gives $Q = u_1$. More generally, choosing $k$ linearly independent $\psi_i \in \operatorname{span}\{ v_1,\dots,v_k \}$ gives $\operatorname{range}(Q) = \operatorname{span}\{u_1,\dots,u_k\}$ and the rank-$k$ projection $QQ^T K$ recovers the exact rank-$k$ SVD of $K$.

Figure~\ref{fig:singular_vectors_of_K} plots the first four right singular vectors of $K$. We see that they are smooth, more closely matching the ``Peak'' test vectors than the Gaussian or Rade\-macher test vectors, explaining the small improvement in error.


\subsubsection{Independence from mesh resolution}
The reconstruction error is independent of the mesh resolution $N$ for fixed $k$ (Section~\ref{sec:mesh_resolution_effect_on_reconstruction_error}). The PDE solution at the boundary is smooth, so finer discretization produces more samples of the same smooth function without introducing new information about the source.

The continuous forward operator $\mathcal{K}$ has rapidly decaying singular values (Section~\ref{sec:ill_posedness}), and the discrete $K$ inherits this decay. As $N$ increases and $\operatorname{rank}(K) = N_b$ grows, the addition singular values are small with little new information.

The target rank $k$ can therefore be selected on a coarse mesh, where rSVD is inexpensive to run, and reused unchanged as the mesh is refined. This is a practical method for tuning $k$ when the final computation on a fine mesh is expensive.


\subsubsection{Estimation of singular values}
Let $\hat P = QQ^T$ with $Q$ produced by Algorithm~\ref{alg:discrete_rsvd}. This is an orthogonal projection onto $\operatorname{range}(Q)$, and $\hat P K$ is the projected matrix from which the rSVD approximation is obtained. The projector satisfies $\| \hat P z \| \le \| z \|$, meaning it shrinks vectors, so $\| \hat P K x  \| \le \|K x \|$ for every $x$. Consequently, $\hat P K$ stretches every vector at most as much as $K$ does, and singular values measure exactly this stretching. This is what we saw inSection~\ref{sec:approximation_of_singular_values}. By choosing a higher oversampling parameter $p$, $\operatorname{range}(Q)$ more closely matches $\operatorname{range}(K)$ (in particular, the $k$ first left singular vectors), and the underestimation reduces. Algorithm~\ref{alg:discrete_rsvd_Kstar} gave a worse approximation (Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction_error_using_the_adjoint_operator}), so the underestimation increases.

The captured subspace $\operatorname{range}(Q)$ depends on the random test vectors $\psi_i$, and so does the degree of underestimation. This is an active area of research. Lemma~4.2 and Theorem~4.3 of Gu \cite{guSubspaceIterationRandomization2015} provide lower bounds for the power-iteration method, which reduces to the plain rSVD used in Algorithm~\ref{alg:discrete_rsvd} when $q = 0$.


\subsubsection{Reconstructions under noisy data}
Choosing $k$ too large worsens reconstruction quality on noisy data (Section~\ref{sec:accuracy_on_noisy_data}), and the target rank acts as a regularization parameter in the same manner as the truncation rank of tSVD.

For a noisy measurement $\tilde y = y + \epsilon$, $y = K x$, we can write the minimum-norm least-squares solution as $x^* = K^+ \tilde y = K^+ y + K^+ \epsilon$ (Section~\ref{sec:the_pseudoinverse}). Expanding in terms of the SVD components,
\begin{equation*}
    x^* = \sum_{i=1}^{N_b}
    \left(\frac{u_i^T y + u_i^T \epsilon}{\sigma_i}\right) v_i,
\end{equation*}
we see that the $i$-th component depends on the exact term $u_i^T y$ and the noise term $u_i^T \epsilon$. Components where $|u_i^T y| \le |u_i^T \epsilon|$ are dominated by noise, and the $\tfrac{1}{\sigma_i}$ factor amplifies this contribution. By the Picard condition \cite{hansen2010discrete}, $u_i^T y$ decays to zero as $i$ increases while $u_i^T \epsilon$ stays approximately constant, so for large $i$ every component is noise-dominated and amplified by a growing $\tfrac{1}{\sigma_i}$.

By truncating the $N_b - k$ terms of the solution, we remove the noisy SVD components. We wish to choose $k$ such that $|u_k^T y| > |u_k^T \epsilon|$ while $|u_{k+1}^T y| < |u_{k+1}^T \epsilon|$, removing only the noise-dominated SVD components $k+1,\, k+2,\dots,\, N_b$.

For the weighted Tikhonov solution, the reconstruction already has some regularization through $\lambda$, but the argument transfers. We found that the optimal $k$ values are approximately equal for both the rSVD and the tSVD. The rank is problem-dependent, and for our problems ranged from $k = 45$ to $k = 85$ by Euclidean norm. The EMD preferred a smaller $k$ ($20$ to $30$), since it is more sensitive to unwanted noise in the reconstruction, while the AUC-IoU generally preferred larger $k$ ($\ge 60$), since it focuses on segmentation overlap, which is less affected by noise.

\subsection{Dynamical low-rank approximation}
\subsubsection{Convergence of DLRA-CG}\label{sec:convergence_of_DLRA_CG}
Replacing the steepest descent search directions with directions $\{D^{(i)}\}$ from CG increased convergence significantly: DLRA-CG reached the relative error $e^{(i)} < 0.85$ approximately $210$ times faster than steepest descent (Section~\ref{sec:rate_of_convergence}).

In an unconstrained setting, the convergence of CG and steepest-descent depends on the condition number $\kappa = \kappa(H)$, with larger $\kappa$ leading to slower convergence \cite{saadIterativeMethodsSparse2003}. If $x^*$ is the unconstrained minimizer of $\Phi(x)$ and $x^{(i)}$ the $i$-th iterate of CG, then by Theorem~6.29 in Saad \cite{saadIterativeMethodsSparse2003},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convergence_of_CG}
    \| x^* - x^{(i)} \|_H \le 2 \left(
        \frac{\sqrt{\kappa} - 1}{\sqrt{\kappa} + 1}
    \right)^i
    \| x^* - x^{(0)} \|_H.
\end{equation}
If $\kappa$ is large, then $\tfrac{\sqrt{\kappa} - 1}{\sqrt{\kappa} + 1} \approx 1 - \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa}}$, compared to the slower convergence of steepest descent which converges as approximately $1 - \tfrac{2}{\kappa}$ \cite{saadIterativeMethodsSparse2003}.

The Hessian $H = F + S$ has a low-rank $F = K^T M_\partial K$ plus a sparse $S = \lambda^2 W^T M W$ structure (Section~\ref{sec:choice_of_preconditioner}). The rapid decay of singular values of $K$ makes $\kappa(H)$ depend on $\lambda$: the small eigenvalues of $H$ are mostly determined by $S$, whose eigenvalues are small when $\lambda$ is small. For example, at $\lambda = 0.1$, $\kappa(H) \approx 10^3$, and at  $\lambda = 10^{-4}$, $\kappa(H) \approx 10^9$.

The same argument partially transfers to DLRA-CG, as demonstrated by Table~\ref{tab:convergence_iterations}, since $\{D^{(i)}\}$ is generated by the same CG recurrence as in the unconstrained case. A formal convergence analysis of DLRA-CG that accounts for the projection and truncation remains an open problem.


\subsubsection{Semi-convergence and restarting}
\input{figures/cg_direction_disagreement}
DLRA-CG shows semi-convergence: initially the relative error reduces, and then it rises again before stalling (Section~\ref{sec:rate_of_convergence}). Restarted DLRA-CG avoids this, but shows slower convergence.

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:restarted_cg}, the DLRA-CG algorithm constructs $(\alpha_i,\, G^{(i)},\, D^{(i)})$ independent of $X^{(i)}$. The search directions $\{D^{(i)}\}$ follow the trajectory of the full-rank solution $Y^{(i)}$, meaning $G^{(i)} = \nabla \Phi(Y^{(i)})$. When $i$ increases, $X^{(i)}$ and $Y^{(i)}$ may drift apart, and $G^{(i)}$ starts disagreeing with the projected gradient 
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{P} G^{(i)} = \mathcal{P}_{X^{(i)}}(\nabla \Phi(X^{(i)})),
\end{equation*}
to such an extent that the relative error $e^{(i)}$ rises again, resulting in semi-convergence.

Restarting sets $D^{(i)} = -\nabla\Phi(X^{(i)})$, so the projected step $\mathcal{P}_{X^{(i)}}(D^{(i)}) = -\mathcal{P}G^{(i)}$ is the descent direction at $X^{(i)}$. As the iterations continue and the CG directions $D^{(i)}$ away from $-\mathcal{P}G^{(i)}$, this alignment gradually breaks down until the next restart. When the restart period $N_r$ is too small, the convergence of DLRA-CG will approach the steepest descent (at $N_r = 1$ they are equal), but choosing $N_r$ too large gives $X^{(i)}$ and $Y^{(i)}$ time to drift apart, so that semi-convergence re-appears.

An adaptive selection of $N_r$ could be considered, for example only restarting once the angle or relative difference between $G^{(i)}$ and the true projected gradient $\mathcal{P}G^{(i)}$ is above some threshold. 


\subsubsection{Rank as a regularization parameter}
\input{figures/outer_product_II.tex}
The low-rank solutions outperformed exact weighted Tikhonov reconstructions (Section~\ref{sec:quality_of_the_low_rank_solutions}), but were sensitive to the choice of $r$: choosing $r$ slightly above optimal gave reconstructions resembling the full-rank case, while choosing $r$ too small introduced spurious sources for Problem~II
(Section~\ref{sec:effect_of_rank_on_reconstructions}).

The sources in Problems~I and II are of low rank and already live on $\mathcal{M}_r$. Each square source factors as an outer product of two vectors, giving a rank-$1$ matrix. The single source of Problem~I is therefore of rank~$1$, and the three sources of Problem~II sum to a matrix of rank~$3$, see Figure~\ref{fig:outer_product_II}. However, we found that a rank-$2$ approximation of this source yielded better reconstruction error. We interpret this as rank truncation acting as additional regularization on top of Tikhonov, and dropping it suppresses smoothing more than it costs in bias.


\subsubsection{Effect of preconditioning}
We saw that precondition sped up convergence, but also raised the reconstruction error (Section~\ref{sec:rate_of_convergence_using_preconditioners}), and the iterates diverged when using an IC + Woodbury preconditioner together with restarting.

The convergence of CG depends on the condition number $\kappa(H)$. By using a preconditioner $P \approx H$, the condition number $\kappa(P^{-1} H)$ is reduced, and the convergence is sped up by Equation~\eqref{eq:convergence_of_CG}. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:convergence_of_DLRA_CG}, $H$ has a large condition number, which makes preconditioning effective.

In the setting of DLRA-PCG, a stronger $P$ accelerates the convergence of the iterates $Y^{(i)}$ so that they reach the full-rank weighted Tikhonov solution in fewer iterations, leaving $X^{(i)}$ further behind on $\mathcal{M}_r$. This raises the error of the final $X^{(i)}$. In the case of the perfect preconditioner $P^{-1} = H^{-1}$, $Y^{(i)}$ reaches the full-rank solution in a single step, and $X^{(i)}$ is moved a single step in the projected direction and truncated to $\mathcal{M}_r$.

When using the IC preconditioner, the higher final error was resolved by restarting. With the IC + Woodbury preconditioner and restarting, however, the iterates $X^{(i)}$ diverge. Since $\mathcal{M}_r$ is non-convex \cite{absilOptimizationAlgorithmsMatrix2008}, $X^{(i)}$ may converge to a local minimum, as demonstrated in Section~\ref{sec:local_minima}, but we have no complete explanation.
